# Copy Principles

What makes a Bumble bio and prompt answers work, with annotated examples. These principles aren't just style preferences — they're grounded in research on how people evaluate dating profiles.

---

## The Core Idea

Profile copy should:
1. Show who they actually are (not who they think they should be)
2. Give people something to respond to (first-message bait)
3. Attract the right people AND repel the wrong ones (filtering)
4. Be memorable (stand out from thousands of generic profiles)

**The research basis:** Creative, original profiles are independently rated as more intelligent, funnier, and more attractive — even controlling for photos (Fiore et al., 2008). People seeking relationships value text quality more than people seeking casual encounters (Sumter et al., 2017), and Bumble's dating-intention badges put both groups on the same app, so the text is what sorts them. In a market where top profiles receive 10-100x the attention of median ones (Bruch & Newman, 2018), differentiation is survival.

**The Bumble-specific reason text matters:** a match expires if nobody sends a first message within 72 hours, and since August 2026 that job belongs to either person. The copy isn't just there to earn a right swipe. It's there to make the first message easy to write.

---

## The Shape of a Bumble Profile

| Slot | Limit | Job |
|------|-------|-----|
| Bio | 300 characters | The strongest hook plus the warmth. Shows first when someone scrolls. Two or three specific sentences, not a list. |
| Prompt 1 | 160 characters | Filter: an opinion or a non-negotiable |
| Prompt 2 | 160 characters | Reveal: a specific ritual, place, or obsession |
| Prompt 3 | 160 characters | Opener bait: something a stranger can ask about in one line |
| Badges | n/a | Facts. Don't repeat them in the bio. |

The bio and prompts should read as one voice. A dry bio and three exclamation-mark prompts is two people.

**Don't spend characters on badges.** Height, job title, city, and star sign are already on the card. A bio that opens "6'1, engineer, London" has spent a fifth of its budget saying nothing new. Use the work badge for the job and the bio for what makes the job interesting.

## Principles

### 1. Specificity > Generic

Generic statements are forgettable and say nothing. Specific details show personality and create hooks.

**Why this works (research):** Toma & Hancock (2012) found that truthful, authentic profiles use more specific, concrete, sensory language — while deceptive profiles default to vague, abstract phrasing. Readers unconsciously detect this: specificity signals honesty. Separately, Construal Level Theory (Trope & Liberman, 2010) shows that concrete language creates psychological closeness — "Jazz Cafe on a weeknight" makes you feel like you're there, while "live music" keeps you at a distance. And Hitsch et al. (2010) showed that specific, verifiable lifestyle details produce significantly higher contact rates.

| Generic (Bad) | Specific (Good) |
|---------------|-----------------|
| "I love live music" | "Jazz Cafe on a weeknight" |
| "I like exploring the city" | "Walking everywhere with nowhere to be" |
| "I appreciate good food" | "Strong opinions about where to find the best ramen" |
| "I have a good sense of humor" | "I hate-watch Saturday Kitchen. Every week." |
| "Family is important to me" | "My 92-year-old great uncle could beat you at pub quiz" |

**The test:** Could 10,000 other people say the exact same thing? Then it's too generic.

### 2. Every Element = First-Message Bait

Every line should make it easy for a match to start a conversation. On Bumble this isn't a nicety; it's the difference between a match and an expired match.

**Why this works (research):** OkCupid's data (Rudder, *Dataclysm*, 2014) showed that messages referencing specific profile content had reply rates 30-40% higher than generic openers. Bumble now flags "hi" and "hey" as low-effort in its own app, which tells you what it sees in its data. The mechanism is simple — a line that gives someone something to say gets a message; a closed statement doesn't.

**Good hooks invite:**
- Questions ("Wait, why do you hate him?")
- Shared opinions ("Oh my god I also hate that")
- Playful challenges ("I bet I could beat your uncle")
- Stories ("Tell me about that")

**Bad hooks are closed:**
- "I love my friends and family" (what do you say to this?)
- "Looking for someone genuine" (okay... and?)
- "Work hard play hard" (meaningless)

**The 60-second test:** read the finished profile as a stranger. If you can't draft a first message in a minute, neither can they.

### 3. Filter In AND Filter Out

The goal isn't maximum likes - it's the right likes.

**Why this works (research):** Strong homophily effects in dating — people preferentially contact those similar on education, cultural tastes, and lifestyle markers (Fiore & Donath, 2005). Specific cultural references function as sorting mechanisms: insiders recognize them and feel affinity, while outsiders self-select out. In a power-law market where quality matches have abundant options (Bruch & Newman, 2018), you don't need volume — you need the right 30-50 people to remember you.

Good copy should:
- Make ideal matches think "yes, this person gets it"
- Make wrong matches think "not for me" (and that's good)

**Filtering mechanisms:**
- Niche references (insiders recognize, others don't)
- Strong opinions (compatible people agree, others bounce)
- Specific humor (right people laugh, wrong people confused)
- Cultural markers (signals belonging to particular worlds)
- The dating-intention badge (the bluntest filter on the app; set it honestly and let it do its job)

**When to dial this down.** The filtering argument assumes an abundant pool. Bruch & Newman's power law holds in any market, but "you only need the right 30-50 people" is only reassuring when 30-50 people exist. Where the viable pool is under about fifty — small metro, narrow age band, minority orientation, or several hard filters stacked — sorting stops paying for itself and starts eating the pool.

Use the thickness band named in Phase 3:

| Mechanism | Thick market | Thin market |
|-----------|--------------|-------------|
| Niche references | Keep — insiders light up | Keep, but carry the hook in the line so outsiders aren't locked out |
| Strong opinions | Keep — bounces the wrong people | State as a preference, not a test |
| Specific humor | Keep | Keep |
| Cultural markers | Keep — signals a world | Keep the marker, lose the shibboleth |

Specificity is never the thing to cut. It signals honesty (Toma & Hancock, 2012) and creates closeness at any pool size. The variable is how much alienation the market can absorb.

### 4. Balance Edge with Warmth

All edge = reads as bitter, negative, hard to be around
All warmth = reads as bland, generic, forgettable

**Why this works (research):** Humor significantly increases perceived attractiveness and is read as a signal of intelligence and social competence (McGee & Shevlin, 2009). But excessive self-deprecation reduces perceived confidence (Lundy, Tan & Cunningham, 1998). Self-disclosure research (Gibbs et al., 2006) shows the most effective profiles balance multiple dimensions — the optimal self-disclosure is moderate depth across breadth of topics. Edge (humor, opinions) signals personality; warmth (family, genuine care) signals relationship potential. You need both.

**The formula:** Dark humor or strong opinions PLUS genuine warmth markers

**Where it goes on Bumble:** the bio is the natural home for warmth because it's read first and it's free text. Put the edge in the prompts. A warm bio over a sharp prompt reads as a whole person; a sharp bio over sharp prompts reads as a grievance.

**Warmth markers:**
- Family connections (especially specific/unusual ones)
- Care for friends
- Genuine enthusiasm for things
- Vulnerability (appropriate level)
- Pets

### 5. Show, Don't Tell

Stating a quality is weak. Demonstrating it is strong.

**Why this works (research):** This maps directly to signaling theory. Donath (2007) distinguishes costly signals (hard to fake, therefore credible) from cheap signals (easy to fake, therefore ignored). Writing "I have dark humor" is a cheap signal — anyone can claim it. Writing "Being nice about Timothée Chalamet" is a costly signal — you have to actually be witty to produce it. Gibbs et al. (2006) confirmed this experimentally: profiles demonstrating personality through specific examples beat trait-adjective lists on honesty ratings, appeal, and contact rates.

| Telling (Weak) | Showing (Strong) |
|----------------|------------------|
| "I have dark humor" | "Being nice about Timothée Chalamet." |
| "I'm adventurous" | "Last month I took a train with no destination" |
| "I'm creative" | "I make exhibition catalogues for galleries" |
| "I value depth" | Reference to specific artists/thinkers/works |
| "I'm fun" | Specific story that demonstrates fun |

## Annotated Examples

### Example 1: The Bio

```
Makes exhibition catalogues for galleries, walks everywhere with nowhere to be,
and will defend brutalist car parks to the death. My 92-year-old great uncle is
my pub quiz partner and he carries the team.
```
(204 characters)

**Why it works:**
- Opens with a costly signal (the catalogues), not a job title — the work badge already has the title
- Three specifics, each an opener: walking, brutalism, the uncle
- Edge ("to the death") landed by warmth (the uncle carries the team)
- No adjectives. Nothing here claims a trait; every clause demonstrates one
- Under budget on purpose — dense beats full (Fiore et al., 2008)

---

### Example 2: The Opinion Hook

**Prompt:** "I get way too excited about"
**Answer:** "Being nice about Timothée Chalamet. Still working on it."

**Why it works:**
- Specific opinion (not generic)
- Polarizing = filters (fans swipe left, haters open)
- Implies dark humor without stating it
- Instant first message (everyone has a take)
- "Still working on it" = self-aware, not bitter
- Memorable (no one else has this exact take)

---

### Example 3: The Warmth + Uniqueness Combo

**Prompt:** "My most controversial opinion is"
**Answer:** "My 92-year-old great uncle could beat you at pub quiz. No offense."

**Why it works:**
- Completely unique (nobody else has this)
- Shows family warmth without being generic
- Playful challenge invites a reply
- "No offense" = dry wit
- Specific enough to be memorable

---

### Example 4: The Cultural Dog Whistle

**Prompt:** "My simple pleasures"
**Answer:** "Brutalist architecture and a good wander with nowhere to be."

**Why it works:**
- "Brutalist architecture" = design/architecture nerd filter
- Specific taste signals cultural engagement
- "Nowhere to be" = relaxed, not performatively busy
- Attracts: people who know what brutalism is
- Repels: people who need activities planned

---

### Example 5: The Guilty Pleasure Reveal

**Prompt:** "The most embarrassing song on my playlist"
**Answer:** "Not a song. Saturday Kitchen, hate-watched live, every single week."

**Why it works:**
- Subverts the prompt (shows wit rather than claiming it)
- Unexpected (not the usual guilty pleasures)
- Specific (not "cooking shows" but THIS one)
- British humor that resonates with right audience
- Easy first message ("Who's your least favorite chef?")

---

### Example 6: The Hidden Gem

**Prompt:** "My hidden gem in the city"
**Answer:** "Jazz Cafe on a Tuesday. Half empty, twice as good."

**Why it works:**
- Specific venue = London cultural marker, homophily filter
- "Tuesday" is a costly signal — you'd have to actually go
- Gives a match a plan, not just a topic ("take me")
- Under 60 characters and complete

---

### Example 7: The Self-Deprecating Flex

**Prompt:** "My most useless talent is"
**Answer:** "Making corporate logos slightly better. It pays, somehow."

**Why it works:**
- Uses humor to mention work without bragging
- Self-deprecating but confident ("it pays")
- Shows they don't take themselves too seriously
- Industry insider joke lands with right people

## What to Avoid

### Generic Phrases (Everyone Says These)
- "Love to laugh"
- "Partner in crime"
- "Looking for my person"
- "Work hard play hard"
- "Living my best life"
- "Not here for hookups"
- "Love adventures"
- "Good vibes only"
- "Fluent in sarcasm"
- "Just ask"

### Closed Statements (No Hook)
- "Family and friends are important to me"
- "I value honesty"
- "Looking for something real"

### Negativity Without Balance
- "No time wasters"
- "If you can't handle me at my worst..."
- "Tired of games"
- "Swipe left if..." followed by a list
- Long lists of dealbreakers

### Vague Descriptors
- "Fun-loving"
- "Easygoing"
- "Down to earth"
- "Old soul"

### Bumble-Specific Waste
- Typing height, job, or city into the bio (the badge shows it)
- Emoji strings as the whole bio
- A prompt that answers the dating-intention badge in words ("looking for something long-term")
- Leaving prompt slots empty because "the bio covers it"

## The Process

1. **Pull from discovery:** What specific details emerged?
2. **Find the unique:** What does no one else have?
3. **Assign the slots:** warmth and the strongest hook to the bio; filter, reveal, opener across the prompts
4. **Create hooks:** How can each element start a conversation?
5. **Check filtering:** Who does this attract/repel? Scale to thickness.
6. **Balance tone:** Edge + warmth in right proportion
7. **Test specificity:** Could 10,000 people say this?
8. **Check the badges:** Does the copy repeat any? Does the intention badge agree with the tone?
9. **Read aloud:** Does it sound like them?
10. **Count characters:** 300 / 160 / 160 / 160

---

## Character Limits

Bumble: 300 characters for the bio, 160 per prompt answer (verify — some app versions show 120), 150 for a Note.

Every word must earn its place. Cut ruthlessly.
