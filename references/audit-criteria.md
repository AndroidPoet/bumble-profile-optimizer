# Profile Audit Criteria

Use this framework to evaluate existing Bumble profiles. Score each category, identify critical issues.

---

## Overall Scoring

| Category | Weight | What to Assess |
|----------|--------|----------------|
| Photos | 40% | Quality, variety, first photo as the card, red flags |
| Bio & Prompts | 30% | Specificity, opener hooks, personality, character budget used |
| Completeness | 15% | Photos, bio, prompts, badges, intention, verification |
| Signaling | 15% | What the profile actually communicates |

**Why these weights:** Photos are weighted highest because research consistently shows they dominate swipe decisions (Tyson et al., 2016; Brand et al., 2012), and Bumble's card layout makes the first photo the entire first impression. Bio and prompts at 30% reflects that text independently predicts attractiveness beyond photos (Fiore et al., 2008), matters more to people seeking serious relationships (Sumter et al., 2017), and on Bumble is what a match uses to write the first message before the 72-hour window closes. Completeness carries a Bumble-specific weight: badges are filterable by other users, so an empty badge removes the profile from searches, and incomplete profiles signal low effort — a cheap signal that repels quality matches (Donath, 2007). The exact percentages are a practical framework, not from a specific study.

**Completion rate:** ___/4 (should be 4/4)

**Character budget used:** bio ___/300, prompts ___/480. Under half is a signal of low effort; the research on length says moderate and dense beats both extremes (Fiore et al., 2008).

### Quality Assessment (Bio and each Prompt)

| Criteria | Points |
|----------|--------|
| Specific (not generic) | +2 |
| Hands a match a first message | +2 |
| Shows personality | +2 |
| Filters for right people | +1 |
| Doesn't restate a badge (job, height, city) | +1 |
| Memorable/unique | +2 |
| **Max per element** | **/10** |

### Generic Phrase Penalties
Deduct points for:
- "Love to laugh" (-2)
- "Partner in crime" (-2)
- "Looking for my person" (-1)
- "Work hard play hard" (-2)
- "Not here for hookups" (-1)
- "Ask me anything" / "Just ask" (-1)
- "Fluent in sarcasm" (-2)
- Lists of generic hobbies or a string of emoji as the whole bio (-1)
- Height, job, or city typed into the bio when the badge already shows it (-1)

**Why generic phrases are penalized:** Toma & Hancock (2012) found that vague, abstract language is a linguistic marker of deception — readers unconsciously process it as less trustworthy. Separately, the von Restorff effect (cognitive psychology) means distinctive items in a set are remembered while common items are forgotten. When someone swipes through hundreds of "love to laugh" bios, generic phrasing makes you invisible.

**The opener test (Bumble-specific):** for the bio and each prompt, ask "could a stranger write a first message from this in under a minute?" If the honest answer for the whole profile is no, the match will expire at 72 hours no matter how good the photos are.

## Signaling Analysis (15%)

This section uses signaling theory (Donath, 2007) — the idea that some profile elements are "costly signals" (hard to fake, therefore credible) and others are "cheap signals" (easy to fake, therefore ignored by discerning matches).

### What Does This Profile Actually Communicate?

**Actual signals** (what it really says):
- [ ] List what the profile actually communicates

**Intention match:** does the dating-intention badge agree with the tone of the bio and photos? A "life partner" badge over a bio that reads as a nightclub flyer sends two messages, and readers believe the cheaper one.

### Signal Quality Check

| Costly Signals (Good) | Present? | Notes |
|-----------------------|----------|-------|
| Effort invested (quality photos, full text budget, all badges) | ☐ | Signals genuine interest in finding someone |

| Cheap Signals (Problematic) | Present? | Notes |
|-----------------------------|----------|-------|
| Low effort (empty prompts, no badges, basic selfies) | ☐ | Signals low investment in finding someone |

## Competitive Position

This matters more than most people realise. Bruch & Newman (2018) found that attention on dating apps follows a power law — the top profiles receive 10-100x more messages than median ones. Hitsch et al. (2010) found that users with the most desirable profiles are also the most selective and the most sensitive to profile quality. The people you want to attract are the people most likely to notice the difference between a good profile and a forgettable one.

### Target Market Alignment
- Who are they trying to attract?
- Does the profile appeal to that demographic specifically, or is it aimed at everyone (which means no one)?
- What would make their target market stop swiping?
- What cultural markers, tone, or references would resonate with that specific audience?
- What's currently missing that their target market would want to see?

### Differentiation
- What makes this profile different from thousands of others?
- Does it contain any costly signals (Donath, 2007) — things that are specific, hard to fake, and demonstrate actual qualities?
- Or is it all cheap signals — generic claims, basic selfies, vague descriptors?
- Apply the 10,000 people test: could 10,000 other people have this exact profile? If yes, it's invisible.
- Would someone remember this profile tomorrow?

## Delivering the Audit

**Lead with positives:**
- "Here's what's working..."
- Find something genuinely good, even if small

**Frame gaps as opportunities:**
- "Here are the opportunities..." not "Here's what's wrong"
- Be honest but kind

**Prioritize:**
- Identify the 3 most impactful changes
- Don't overwhelm with everything at once
- On Bumble, an unset intention badge or an unverified photo is often the cheapest big win. Say so.
